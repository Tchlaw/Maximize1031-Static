Most 1031 exchange guides assume one sale and one purchase. But if you own several properties, the questions are different: _Which_ assets should you trade? In what order? How do you line up the deadlines when three closings are moving at once? And how do you buy the property you really want before someone else does?

This guide is for owners who think about their real estate as a portfolio rather than a collection of individual deals.

**The short answer:** A portfolio 1031 strategy treats each exchange as a rebalancing move. You sell the properties that have stopped earning their keep — because the value-add is finished, or because too much equity is trapped in a low-yielding trophy asset — and reinvest the proceeds into properties that better fit your goals, deferring both capital gains tax and depreciation recapture. Success comes down to three things: choosing the right structure (one-for-many, many-for-one, or a reverse exchange), controlling which 45- and 180-day clock you are on, and lining up replacement properties _before_ you sell.

**Key takeaways**

- Rank your properties by **return on equity**, not return on cost. A property can look great on paper and still be the weakest asset in your portfolio.
- When you sell several properties in **one** exchange, the 45-day and 180-day deadlines run from the **first** sale. Later sales eat into a clock that is already running.
- Running **separate** exchanges gives each sale its own clock, but each exchange must balance value and debt on its own.
- A **reverse exchange** lets you buy the replacement first and sell later — often the best tool when the property you want is scarce and the ones you own are easy to sell.
- In **California**, the state keeps a claim on deferred gain even if you exchange into another state, and a 1031 does not carry over your Prop 13 tax base.

## Why portfolio owners use 1031 exchanges to trade assets

Investors with one property usually exchange to “trade up.” Owners with a portfolio exchange for more varied reasons:

- **The value-add is done.** You bought, renovated, re-tenanted and raised rents. The remaining upside is small, but the equity is large. Selling and redeploying that equity into the next opportunity is the classic _buy, improve, exchange, repeat_ cycle.
- **A trophy asset is under-earning.** A well-located building can be a source of pride and a drag on returns. Trading it for a property with a higher, more durable yield may increase cash flow, although tenant, market and financing risks still need to be compared.
- **Consolidation.** Several small properties mean several roofs, several tenants and several sets of books. Exchanging them into one larger asset can cut management time and often improves financing terms.
- **Diversification.** The opposite move: break one large, concentrated position into several properties across markets or property types.
- **Changing markets or property types.** Any real property held for investment or business use can generally be exchanged for any other — apartments for industrial, retail for land, California for Texas. (See [What You Can Actually Buy in a 1031 Exchange](/blog/2025-11-13-What-You-Can-Actually-Buy-In-a-1031-Exchange).)
- **Lighter management.** Moving from hands-on residential to triple-net (NNN) leased property, or into fractional and fund-style structures, can free up time.
- **Estate planning.** Deferring gain throughout your lifetime and passing property to heirs at a stepped-up basis is a long-term strategy many families build their portfolio around.

## Step 1: Sort your holdings into hold, harvest and trade

Before you talk to a broker, look at your whole portfolio side by side. For each property, write down:

- **Current value and equity** (value minus debt)
- **Net operating income (NOI)** and the **return on equity** (NOI after debt service, divided by your equity — not your original purchase price)
- **Remaining upside** — is there still a rent gap, an entitlement play or a repositioning to finish?
- **Built-in gain and depreciation taken** — how much tax a sale would trigger, including depreciation recapture
- **Debt terms** — rate, maturity, and any prepayment penalty or defeasance cost
- **Management burden** — time, headaches and capital needs

Then sort each one:

- **Hold:** still has upside, strong return on equity, or a reason to keep it (for example, an exceptionally low property-tax base or a below-market loan).
- **Harvest:** the work is done and the equity can earn more elsewhere. These are your relinquished-property candidates.
- **Trade up or out:** properties you would not buy today at today’s price.

A useful gut check: _If I had this property’s equity in cash today, would I buy this property with it?_ If the answer is no, it belongs on the trade list.

One more filter: not every sale should be an exchange. If a property has losses, little gain, or you want the cash, paying the tax — or pairing a purchase with cost segregation — may be better. (See [When Not to Do a 1031 Exchange](/blog/2025-11-29-when-not-to-do-a-1031-exchange-cost-seg-vs-deferral).)

## Step 2: Choose the right exchange structure

Once you know what you are selling and what you want to own, pick the structure that fits. Most portfolio moves fall into one of five patterns.

|Structure|Best for|Key timing issue|
|---|---|---|
|**One-for-one**|A simple swap of one property for another|Standard 45/180-day clock from the sale|
|**One-for-many**|Diversifying a large or concentrated asset|All replacements must be identified by day 45 and closed by day 180|
|**Many-for-one**|Consolidating several properties into one|If run as one exchange, both deadlines run from the _first_ sale|
|**Reverse (buy first)**|Securing a scarce replacement before you sell|The exchange accommodation titleholder (EAT) can hold the property for up to 180 days|
|**Improvement (build-to-suit)**|Using exchange funds to improve the replacement|Only improvements completed within 180 days count toward the exchange|

The basics — the qualified intermediary, the 45-day and 180-day rules, and boot — are covered in [Understanding 1031 Exchange Basics](/blog/2025-10-26-understanding-1031-exchange-basics). The rest of this guide focuses on what changes when more than one property is involved.

## Step 3: Get the timing right

Timing is where portfolio exchanges succeed or fail. The baseline rules:

- You have **45 calendar days** from the sale to identify replacement property in writing.
- You have **180 calendar days** from the sale to close — **or until your tax return is due for that year, if earlier**. For an individual with an April tax-return deadline, a late-year sale can leave fewer than 180 days unless the return is extended.
- Weekends and holidays count, and the deadlines are not extended except in limited cases such as federally declared disasters.

With multiple properties, the question is **which clock applies**. There are four common scenarios.

### Scenario A: You sell two properties and buy two replacements

The simplest approach is to treat these as **two separate exchanges**. Each sale has its own qualified intermediary account, its own 45-day identification deadline, and its own 180-day closing deadline.

- **Advantage:** If one sale is delayed or one purchase falls through, the other exchange is not affected.
- **Trade-off:** Each exchange is tested on its own for value and debt. Buying too much in one does not make up for buying too little in the other.

### Scenario B: You sell several properties and buy one (many-for-one)

Consolidation is the trickiest timing problem, because one purchase has to absorb the proceeds of several sales. You have three options.

**Option 1 — One combined exchange.** Under the Treasury regulations, when you sell more than one property as part of the same exchange on different dates, both the 45-day and 180-day periods run from the **earliest** sale. If your first property sells on March 1 and your last sells on May 15, you have until April 15 to identify and until August 28 to close — regardless of when the last sale closes.

This works when the sales close together. It gets risky when they are spread out, because every week of delay on the later sales comes out of a clock that is already running.

**Option 2 — Separate exchanges into the same property.** Each sale runs as its own exchange, and each identifies the same replacement. At closing, funds from each exchange account go toward the purchase. This keeps one failed sale from contaminating the others. But in practice the purchase still has to close before the _earliest_ exchange’s deadline, so it does not buy you much extra time.

**Option 3 — Reverse exchange first.** Buy the replacement before you sell. An exchange accommodation titleholder (EAT) takes title to the new property and “parks” it. Under the safe harbor, the QEAA must be documented within five business days; you then have 45 days to identify which properties you will sell and 180 days to complete the exchange. You can identify more than one relinquished property.

For consolidation, this is often the cleanest answer: the large property you want is usually harder to find than buyers for your smaller properties. Lock up the hard part first.

### Scenario C: You sell one property and buy several (one-for-many)

One sale means one clock. Every replacement must be identified by day 45 and closed by day 180. The challenge here is identification, not timing: if you plan to buy three properties, the three-property rule leaves you no backups (more on this in Step 4). Line up your targets — and your financing — before you list.

### Scenario D: Combine a reverse and a forward exchange

Larger portfolio moves often mix the two. For example, park a hard-to-find replacement in a reverse exchange tied to one sale, while running a standard forward exchange on other sales into easier-to-find properties. Each exchange keeps its own clock and its own paperwork. (Our [Reverse 1031 Exchange Guide](/blog/2025-07-09-reverse-1031-exchange-guide-california) explains how the EAT structure works.)

**What a reverse exchange costs you:** higher fees than a forward exchange, a lender willing to lend to the EAT (not all are), and the cash or credit to buy before you sell. Under the IRS safe harbor, the parking period cannot exceed 180 days, and you generally cannot park a property you already owned in the prior 180 days.

### Watch the calendar at year-end

If you sell in the fourth quarter, your 180 days can be cut short by your tax-return due date. Extend your return so you get the full period. And if an exchange might fail, the timing of a late-year sale can affect _which year_ you report the gain. (See [A Bona Fide 1031 Attempt Can Push Tax into Next Year](/blog/2025-10-26-bona-fide-1031-failed-exchange-tax-deferral).)

## Step 4: Identify replacement properties the right way

Identification must be **in writing, signed by you, and delivered** to your qualified intermediary (or another permitted party) by midnight of day 45. Each property must be described unambiguously — a street address or legal description. You can change your list in writing until day 45; after that, it is locked.

You must satisfy one of three rules:

- **Three-property rule:** Identify up to three properties of any value.
- **200% rule:** Identify any number of properties, as long as their combined value does not exceed 200% of the combined value of everything you sold in that exchange.
- **95% rule:** Identify more than that, but then you must actually buy at least 95% of the value you identified. In practice this is rarely a safe plan.

**What changes for portfolio owners:**

- **Buying several properties uses up the three-property rule fast.** If you plan to buy three, you have no backups. The 200% rule is usually the better fit for one-for-many exchanges — for example, a $6 million sale lets you identify up to $12 million of property, in any number of pieces.
- **In a many-for-one exchange, the 200% rule is measured against all the properties sold in that exchange combined** — which gives you more room to name backups for one large purchase.
- **Separate exchanges each get their own identification list.** Keep them clearly labeled so a property identified for one exchange is not confused with another.
- **Build the shortlist before you list.** The 45-day window is short, and the best replacement properties are rarely available on your schedule. Many experienced owners have letters of intent or a reverse exchange in place before the first sale closes.
- **Always name a backup.** Inspections, financing and sellers all fall through. A backup identified on day 30 is worth far more than a perfect target lost on day 50.

## Step 5: Balance value and debt across every sale

To defer all of your gain, the general rule is to **buy at least as much as you sold** (net of selling costs) and **reinvest all of your net equity**. Any cash you take out is taxable “cash boot.” If your new debt is less than the debt you paid off and you don’t make up the difference with cash, the shortfall is taxable “mortgage boot.”

With multiple properties, structure matters:

- **In one combined exchange,** value, equity and debt are measured across all the properties together. Overbuying one replacement can offset underbuying another.
- **In separate exchanges,** each exchange stands on its own. Extra value in exchange #1 does not cover a shortfall in exchange #2.
- **If you want cash out, plan for it deliberately.** Boot generally triggers recognized gain up to the amount of realized gain; depreciation recapture and other tax rules can affect the calculation. Some owners instead complete the exchange and refinance the replacement property afterward — talk with your tax advisor about the timing, because refinancing right before or during an exchange can raise boot questions.
- **Track it in one place.** A simple spreadsheet with each relinquished and replacement property, its value, debt paid off, new debt and equity will save you from surprises at closing.

Once funds are with the qualified intermediary, how they can be spent is tightly limited. (See [What Can You Spend Exchange Funds On?](/blog/2026-04-09-permissible-use-of-exchange-funds))

## California owners: what's different

California follows the federal 1031 rules for real property, so a valid federal exchange generally defers California tax too. But California owners repositioning a portfolio should plan for five state-specific issues.

**1. California follows your deferred gain out of state.** If you exchange California property for property in another state, California keeps the right to tax the gain that built up while you owned the California property. You must file **FTB Form 3840** every year you hold the out-of-state replacement property, and when you eventually sell in a taxable sale, California taxes its share — even if you no longer live here. This matters for portfolio owners consolidating into lower-tax states. (See [Selling California Property and Buying Out of State?](/blog/2026-04-15-selling-california-property-and-buying-out-of-state))

**2. Withholding on every sale.** California generally requires withholding of 3⅓% of the sales price on real estate sales unless an exemption applies. In an exchange, you claim the exemption on **Form 593**, and your escrow and qualified intermediary coordinate it. If the exchange fails or produces boot, withholding can apply. With several sales running at once, make sure each escrow has the right paperwork.

**3. No Prop 13 carryover.** A 1031 defers income tax; it does not carry your property-tax base to the new property. A California replacement property is generally reassessed at its current fair market value when ownership changes, unless a separate exclusion applies. For a trophy asset bought decades ago, the property-tax increase on the replacement can be large — build it into your return-on-equity comparison.

**4. Transfer taxes still apply.** City and county transfer taxes are not deferred by a 1031. In the City of Los Angeles, Measure ULA adds a substantial tax on sales above certain value thresholds (adjusted annually). For high-value sales, this can be one of the biggest costs of the move — and a reason to compare holding against selling carefully.

**5. Any boot is taxed at California rates.** California taxes capital gains at ordinary income tax rates rather than a separate preferential capital gains rate. Taking cash out of a California exchange is more expensive than in most states, so be deliberate about how much boot you take.

## Common portfolio traps

**The same taxpayer must sell and buy.** The taxpayer that sells the relinquished property must be the one that acquires the replacement. Portfolios often hold properties in different LLCs, and that matters:

- A **single-member LLC** that is disregarded for tax purposes is treated as its owner, so you can usually sell from one and buy in another you own alone.
- A **multi-member LLC** taxed as a partnership is its own taxpayer. Proceeds from Partnership LLC A cannot simply be combined with your personal proceeds to buy one property in your name.
- If partners want different outcomes — one exchanges, another cashes out — the restructuring has to happen well before the sale. (See [How to Structure Partner-Owned Real Estate for 1031 Flexibility](/blog/2026-04-27-how-to-structure-partner-owned-real-estate-for-1031-flexibility).)

**Related-party rules.** Family members and entities you control are “related parties.” Exchanges with them are not banned, but special rules apply: generally both sides must hold the exchanged properties for two years, and buying your replacement property _from_ a related party that cashes out can disqualify the exchange. Having the related party exchange as well does not automatically make the structure qualify; have your advisers review the anti-abuse rules and exceptions. Moving properties between family entities is exactly where portfolio owners trip over this.

**Dealer risk when the value-add is “done.”** A 1031 only applies to property held for investment or for use in a business — not property held for sale. If you buy, renovate and sell quickly, the IRS can argue you were a dealer flipping inventory. There is no fixed safe-harbor holding period, but a longer hold, a real rental history and an investment-focused paper trail all help. The same applies to your replacement property: don’t buy it with a plan to flip it.

**Financing and loan exits.** Prepayment penalties, defeasance costs and lender approval timelines on several properties at once can derail a 180-day schedule. Start loan conversations early, and confirm your new lender is comfortable with your exchange structure — especially in a reverse exchange.

**Doing it all in one year.** Bunching several sales into one calendar year can help with planning, but it also concentrates risk: one failed exchange can create a large tax bill in a single year.

## Worked example: repositioning a four-property portfolio

_This example is hypothetical. Numbers are rounded and ignore transaction costs, transfer taxes and financing fees; actual reinvestment requirements must be calculated from the closing statements._

Elena owns four California properties:

|Property|Value|Debt|Equity|Notes|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|A — 12-unit apartment building, Los Angeles|$5.0M|$2.6M|$2.4M|Renovated and re-leased; the value-add is done|
|B — Retail building, Santa Monica (her “trophy”)|$8.0M|$1.5M|$6.5M|Great location, about a 3.8% yield|
|C — Duplex, Glendale|$1.4M|$0.4M|$1.0M|High maintenance for its size|
|D — Mixed-use building, Pasadena|—|—|—|Still has upside — **hold**|

**Her goal:** more income, less management, and some diversification outside California.

**Her plan:** sell A, B and C; buy one larger industrial building and two NNN-leased retail properties. Together, the sales would trigger more than $8 million of gain and depreciation recapture if she simply sold.

### Exchange 1: Reverse exchange (B into the industrial building)

The multi-tenant industrial building she wants in the Inland Empire is competitive, so she uses a reverse exchange to buy it first.

- **Feb 1, 2027:** An EAT acquires the $9.0M industrial building, funded with a bridge loan and an advance from Elena. Her 45- and 180-day clocks start.
- **Mar 18 (day 45):** She identifies the Santa Monica building (B) as the property she will sell.
- **Jun 15:** B sells for $8.0M. The $6.5M of net proceeds repays her advance and pays down the bridge loan, leaving $2.5M of permanent debt.
- **By Jul 31 (day 180):** The EAT transfers the industrial building to Elena, completing the exchange.

She sold $8.0M and bought $9.0M, reinvested all $6.5M of equity, and her new debt ($2.5M) exceeds the debt paid off ($1.5M). No boot.

### Exchange 2: Combined forward exchange (A + C into two NNN properties)

The apartment building and the duplex are run as **one combined forward exchange** because their sales are expected to close within a few weeks of each other.

- **Apr 15:** A sells for $5.0M. The combined exchange’s clock starts.
- **May 20:** C sells for $1.4M — inside the same window.
- **May 30 (day 45 from A’s sale):** Elena identifies three NNN properties in Arizona and Texas (three-property rule).
- **Aug 20 and Sep 30:** She closes on two of them, for $3.5M and $3.3M.
- **Oct 12 (day 180):** Deadline — already met.

She sold $6.4M and bought $6.8M, reinvested all $3.4M of equity, and took on $3.4M of new debt against the $3.0M paid off. No boot.

**Why not run C as its own exchange?** She could have. If C’s sale had been likely to slip past A’s 45-day mark, separate exchanges would have been safer, so C’s delay couldn’t jeopardize A’s timeline.

### The result

|Metric|Before (A, B, C)|After (industrial + 2 NNN)|
|---|---|---|
|Properties|3 (all California)|3 (1 California, 2 out of state)|
|Total value|$14.4M|$15.8M|
|Approximate NOI|~$585K|~$920K|
|Management|Hands-on residential and retail|Mostly NNN and industrial|
|Tax due now|$0|$0|

NOI is before debt service, and Elena now carries more debt, so her cash flow increase is smaller than the NOI jump. She will also file California Form 3840 each year for the Arizona and Texas properties, and California will tax that deferred gain when they are eventually sold in a taxable sale.

## Portfolio 1031 exchange checklist

**6–12 months before**

- Rank every property by return on equity, remaining upside and built-in gain
- Decide which properties to hold, harvest or trade
- Confirm how each property is titled (which LLC, which taxpayer)
- Restructure any partnerships where partners want different outcomes
- Review loan prepayment penalties and lender timelines

**Before listing**

- Choose the structure: separate exchanges, one combined exchange, reverse, or a mix
- Map every 45- and 180-day deadline on one calendar, including your tax-return due date
- Build a replacement shortlist, with backups
- Line up financing for replacements (and for the EAT, if doing a reverse)
- Engage a qualified intermediary and loop in your CPA and attorney

**During the exchange**

- Sign exchange documents _before_ each sale closes
- Deliver written identification by day 45 — one list per exchange
- Track value, equity and debt for each exchange so there is no boot you didn’t plan for
- California: confirm Form 593 for each sale

**After**

- Report each exchange on IRS Form 8824
- California: file Form 3840 each year for out-of-state replacement property
- Update your portfolio model — and start planning the next move

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I sell two properties and buy one property in a 1031 exchange?

Yes. You can run it as one combined exchange, where both deadlines run from the first sale, or as separate exchanges that each fund part of the purchase. If the replacement is hard to find, a reverse exchange lets you buy it first and sell your properties afterward.

### When does the 45-day clock start if I sell several properties?

If the sales are part of one exchange, both the 45-day identification period and the 180-day exchange period start on the date of the **first** sale. If each sale is a separate exchange, each has its own clock.

### Can I sell one property and buy several?

Yes. All replacements must be identified within 45 days and acquired within 180 days of the sale. The 200% rule is usually the most practical identification rule when you plan to buy several properties.

### Can I do a reverse exchange and a forward exchange at the same time?

Yes. Many portfolio repositionings pair a reverse exchange for a hard-to-find replacement with forward exchanges for the rest. Each exchange is documented and tracked separately, with its own deadlines.

### Can I exchange a property I own personally for one owned by my LLC?

Only if the LLC is disregarded for tax purposes (for example, a single-member LLC you own). If the LLC is a partnership or corporation for tax purposes, it is a different taxpayer and the exchange will not qualify as structured.

### What happens if I trade California property for property in another state?

The exchange can still defer tax, but California keeps the right to tax the gain that accrued in California. You must file FTB Form 3840 each year, and California taxes that gain when the out-of-state property is eventually sold in a taxable sale.

### How long do I need to own a property before exchanging it?

There is no fixed holding period in the tax code. What matters is that the property was held for investment or business use — not for resale. A longer hold and genuine rental operations make that easier to show.
